How is the reading calculated?

The model calculates illuminance at the eye with the sensor open and with the selected attachment fitted. A bright patch contributes more than a dark one. Distance, the orientation of the patch and the angle at which light strikes the sensor also matter. The percentage is the reading with the attachment divided by the open reading for the same gaze direction.

The reference sets the light level throughout the model. It is the open reading at the starting position in experiment 01, looking straight ahead with reflections on. The default is 250 lx. Changing it scales the illuminance readings in every experiment by the same factor. The percentages stay the same.

Two lighting scenes in the same classroom

A spectrometer measured light at eye level in a classroom. Panels supplied most of the light in the morning. Spotlights made a larger contribution in the evening. Each scene was measured twice, first with an open sensor and then with a radiance hood.

The hood reduced the morning reading by 11% and the evening reading by 43%. Even in the same room, the lighting scene changes which directions contribute to the reading. Zauner et al. (2023), Fig. 4, scenes 11 and 13

Melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance (melanopic EDI) is the illuminance of standard daylight D65 that would produce the same melanopic stimulus. The percentages in the interactive room come from the geometric teaching model.

Guided introduction

What determines the reading?

When the person looks straight ahead, the sensor at the eye sits 1.20 m above the floor. Negative angles tilt the gaze towards the desk, positive angles towards the ceiling. As the person nods, the neck moves at the shoulders and the head tilts at the top of the neck. The eye shifts slightly in space. The sensor and any attachment follow the eye. The figure uses a simplified model of this movement.

Each surface patch in front of the sensor contributes according to its luminance, apparent size and angle of incidence. Light that strikes the sensor at a grazing angle contributes less.

Open measurement
In a hemispherical measurement the sensor accepts light from the forward hemisphere, extending 90° to either side. Straight ahead is 0° elevation.
Radiance hood
CIE S 026/E:2018 uses the term “radiance hood” for a baffle fitted over a sensor. It blocks light from outside the chosen field of view. Annex A.6, Table A.4 gives typical limits for low light levels indoors. They extend vertically from −70° to +50°. Horizontally, the combined field of both eyes spans 180°. These angles are relative to the current line of sight. The model follows Figure 1b of Zauner et al. for the lateral outline. Two horizontal lines bound the field in the equidistant angular diagram. This idealised boundary applies to both direct and reflected light. Figure 6d shows the slightly curved opening of the physical attachment, which accounts for shading across the entire diffuser. CIE S 026/E:2018, Annex A.6 · Zauner et al. (2023), Figs. 1b and 6d

Direct and reflected light

The ceiling panel is 1.20 m long and 0.90 m wide. The wall panel and daylight window occupy the same opening in the front wall. It is 2.60 m wide and 1.83 m high. The lower edge is level with the desktop at 72 cm, and the top is 2.55 m above the floor. Both sources emit diffuse light and give the same reading for the same luminous flux.

With the front wall light off, the ceiling panel supplies all the luminous flux. Turning the wall light back on restores the previous split. In “Room size”, the luminous ceiling keeps the same luminance. Turning on the wall source adds the same output as at the starting position in experiment 01.

By default, the floor diffusely reflects 20% of the incident light, the walls and desk each reflect 50%, and the ceiling reflects 80%. The glass face of the wall panel and window reflects 8% in the mirror direction. The model uses this value at all angles of incidence, based on a 4 mm sheet of uncoated clear glass. The figure and chair illustrate the seating position. Pilkington Optifloat™ Clear, technical data

Illuminance and angles

Illuminance at the eye is given in lux (lx). The reference is the open hemispherical reading at the starting position in experiment 01, with a level gaze and the default reflectances. It starts at 250 lx and can be changed in the results panel. The reading with the selected attachment is also shown as a percentage of the open reading at the same position and gaze direction. Each surface contributes according to its luminance, apparent size and angle of incidence.

In the sensor view, the centre is 0° and every point on the rim is 90° from the line of sight. A diameter spans 180°. The 30° and 60° rings show angular distance from the centre. In this equidistant projection, image radius is proportional to angle. The section view shows the vertical plane through the line of sight. Light from surfaces to either side also contributes to the reading.

Calculation and independent checks

R integrates direct light over the emitting surfaces. Each patch contributes according to its luminance, the cosine factors at source and receiver, and the inverse square of the distance. For narrow visible ceiling strips and the wall source, the model integrates analytically across the width. Along the other dimension it uses adaptive integration for the ceiling and Gaussian quadrature for the wall source.

For diffuse interreflections, deterministic ray samples give view factors between 39 surface patches. Reciprocal exchange areas are balanced to conserve energy. A finer subdivision captures the first reflection and shading by the desktop. Light falling directly on the desk from the window or wall panel can be calculated analytically even when the desk touches the wall. At the glass, R follows rays in the mirror direction. It integrates the path from source to glass to eye using a virtual image of the source and the finite glass opening. The radiosity solution includes further paths via matte surfaces and glass. The display uses values precomputed in R. Room colours identify materials and light sources. The animation samples possible light paths across the emitting surfaces. An open circle marks a reflection. The frequency of the animated paths is illustrative. The results panel gives the calculated illuminance at the eye in lux.

Numerical comparison

All values below use 250 lx for the open reference reading in experiment 01. Direct light was independently checked from the receiver side in 27 configurations. More than a million directions per configuration sample the open sensor and both attachments. The largest difference from integration over the emitting surfaces is 0.135 lx.

For reflected light, an independently implemented R path tracer follows two million random paths backwards from the eye per scene. Eight separate runs give the estimate and its standard error. A shading disc for lighting design

In rooms lit from above, high ceiling lights often account for much of the light excluded by the radiance hood. A matte black disc lets a lighting designer test the contribution from those directions.

Aim the sensor straight ahead at eye level. Centre the horizontal disc 10 cm above the measurement point. With a diameter of 16.8 cm, its rim is at +50° as seen from the sensor. Tilt the disc and sensor together when the gaze changes.

Compare Open and Disc with the gaze held steady. The difference in lux tells you how much light reaches the sensor from above +50°. The disc admits light from below. The radiance hood used by Zauner et al. also limits light from below. Zauner et al. (2023), Figs. 1 and 6

Adds Disc to the field-of-view choices and to playback in experiment 01.
